The most beautiful thing about traditional B2B SaaS GTM is that you have 500 different tools feeding you the exact signal to go after an account via an API that can already be integrated with Claude code with a single click.

I knew the machinery. Find the market, find the accounts, enrich the people, find a reason one prospect deserves attention before another, then automate as much of it as possible. I learnt all the tricks and hoops at Huntd and worked with growth teams at companies like Rho, Hyperbound and Peec.

Then I joined a YC Backed Physical AI company Relling selling automation into manufacturers.

The first lists looked normal. Automotive suppliers, aerospace companies, machine shops, employee counts, operations leaders. Everything had the reassuring appearance of GTM.

But soon I realised none of this actually meant anything.

Take Ford. Knowing Ford is a manufacturer is not proprietary intelligence. Even knowing the right executive gets you only so far because the physical problem a robotics company solves does not exist at "Ford." It exists in a building.

One facility might be fine. Another under the same logo might be struggling to keep an operation staffed while adding capacity and quietly creating the economics for automation.

That became real when I found a facility where the same production role had effectively been open for 126 days and reposted 13 times.

"Ford is a good account" suddenly felt like the wrong scouting report. "This exact plant has been trying to fill this exact job for four months" was much more interesting.

That was when I started thinking about *Moneyball*.

## The wrong scouting report

The interesting part of Moneyball was never baseball plus spreadsheets. Baseball already had statistics. Oakland's edge came from valuing ordinary information differently from everybody else.

Industrial GTM is the same thing just a different stadium.

The standard account model gives me company size, revenue, industry and headcount. Useful, obvious, and available to every competitor.

The stranger information looks much less impressive: a production job that refuses to die, a second shift, a plant expansion buried in a county announcement, a SAM.gov opportunity, a USAspending award, a PE acquisition, an integrator already working inside the facility.

None of these is a robotics lead. Good.

My first attempt at this was hiring. Factories are remarkably generous about publishing their problems on careers pages. They just do not describe them as problems.

No plant posts, "The economics of manual material handling have deteriorated. Robotics vendors, please call Susan."

It posts another Material Handler opening.

Then it posts it again and again and AGAIN.

One opening tells me almost nothing. Hiring can mean growth, turnover, a terrible shift or a terrible recruiter. But persistence interested me, so I built a basic system around production hiring and started looking for facilities where the pain stuck around.

Even with narrow coverage, the first run surfaced 300+ US plants.

For about fifteen minutes, I felt like I had discovered 300 robotics opportunities. I had not. I had discovered 300 facilities where something looked annoying.

That was the useful part. Hiring was not the signal. It was one stat on the baseball card.

## Ten bad signals can make one good bet

![A corkboard mapping one Ford facility: hiring persistence, a capacity expansion, a SAM.gov opportunity, a USAspending award, PE ownership and an integrator already in the plant, all pinned to the same building](/blog/moneyball-corkboard.jpg)

Once I stopped expecting hiring to do all the work, the internet became much more useful.

A plant struggles to fill the same production role for months. Mildly interesting. Then the facility announces another production line. More interesting. Then there is a government award connected to increased output, management starts talking about backlog, and an integrator in your ecosystem already works there.

Now I care.

No single datapoint caused that change. The overlap did.

The signals are noisy in different ways. A stale job post can fool me. So can an optimistic expansion announcement. A partnership can mean two executives took a photograph together at a conference. A government award may have only a vague relationship with the factory I care about.

But when datasets created for completely different reasons keep pointing at the same building, the coincidence becomes harder to explain away.

Plant expansions tell me where physical capacity is changing. Job descriptions can tell me what work actually happens inside the facility: load these parts, inspect these assemblies, tend this machine, deburr these components. The careers page is accidentally giving you part of the process map.

Then I ended up in government procurement, which was not where my younger self expected GTM engineering to lead.

SAM.gov is one of those websites where the federal government appears to be testing how badly you really want the lead. But opportunities and solicitations can show demand forming before the money moves. USAspending gives you the other side, letting you work backwards from money that has already landed.

Neither tells you "buy robot." That would be suspiciously convenient.

But a government award becomes more interesting when the manufacturer is expanding capacity. The expansion becomes more interesting when the likely facility has persistent production openings. Those openings become more interesting when management is talking about backlog.

At some point this stopped feeling like lead generation and started feeling slightly more like Lester Freamon in *The Wire*, except the corkboard was full of industrial parks.

Private equity changed the model again. Initially I treated PE ownership like normal enrichment.

Company X → Owner Y

Wonderful. Another column.

Then it clicked that ownership could be distribution. If a PE firm owns several manufacturers with similar processes and you solve a painful automation problem at one, the portfolio can become a route into the others.

Integrators, distributors and OEM partnerships started looking the same way. I had thought of them as enrichment. In reality, they could be the way into the plant.

An integrator that has serviced a facility for ten years knows things my contact database never will. They know the equipment, the process, who actually makes decisions, and probably which automation project went horribly wrong in 2021.

A corporate cold email and an introduction from that integrator are both technically "routes in" in roughly the same way walking and being fired from a cannon are both transportation.

By this point, I had gone from companies and contacts to plants, pain, triggers and relationships.

This was supposed to be lead generation. Somewhere along the way it became *Factorio*.

## SaaS has databases. Industrial GTM has PDFs.

![A desk buried in printouts: SAM.gov contract opportunities, USAspending award summaries, plant expansion lists and metalforming job reports, next to a laptop running a spreadsheet](/blog/moneyball-pdfs.jpg)

The system I eventually built at Relling started feeding on 15+ public signals.

Most are mediocre individually. That is precisely why I like them. One source gives me trivia. Several independent sources give me a hypothesis. Enough of them pointing at the same building means somebody should probably make the call.

Coming from SaaS makes the contrast funny. People data, funding, job changes, technographics, intent, competitor usage, all of it has been packaged somewhere. If you want to combine everything, Clay will happily let you create a table complex enough that future archaeologists may classify it as a religious object.

Industrial GTM lives on another technological timeline.

Useful information sits across job boards, local newspapers, state websites, PE portfolios, partner directories, SAM.gov, USAspending and PDFs created by people who appear to consider responsive web design an unresolved debate.

I used to think this was the frustrating part. Now I think it is the opportunity.

If everybody can buy the same company and people data, that data stops being an edge. Your competitor also has Apollo. Your competitor also has Sales Navigator.

The physical economy has not been flattened into the same clean data layer yet. There is no neat record that says: this exact facility performs these processes, has these labor problems, is adding capacity, received this new demand, works with these integrators and has these people responsible for fixing it.

If that existed, I would happily pay for it and reclaim several hobbies.

It mostly does not.

Which is why the mess is valuable.

Physical AI is moving quickly. Robotics companies are getting better at automating variable, messy physical work that historically stayed manual because humans remain annoyingly capable general-purpose robots.

The GTM infrastructure has not caught up.

We are getting very good at answering, "Can the robot do this?"

We are still surprisingly bad at answering, "Which plants have this problem badly enough right now that somebody should call them?"

That mismatch is what pulled me in.

There is a slightly inconvenient ending to Moneyball if your job depends on information advantages. Eventually everybody learns. The weird statistics become normal statistics. The cheap players stop being cheap. The edge gets competed away.

Industrial GTM will probably go the same way. Plant hiring persistence, expansions, government demand, ownership and relationship graphs will eventually become normal CRM fields. A rep will see that a facility has been trying to fill the same role for 126 days directly inside Salesforce.

It will not feel clever. It will just be data.

We are not there yet.

For now, the scouting reports are still scattered across job boards, government databases, county announcements, partner pages, PE portfolios and documents called `FINAL_Project_Update_v7_USE_THIS_ONE.pdf`.

The market still tends to value the logo more than the building, and ten mediocre signals can still tell you something your competitor has not noticed.

I joined Relling thinking I was going to build GTM for robots.

Instead, I somehow ended up playing Moneyball with factories :)
